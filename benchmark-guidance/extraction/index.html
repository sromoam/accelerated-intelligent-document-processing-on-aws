Key Information Extraction Benchmarks

Section titled “Key Information Extraction Benchmarks”

Extraction fills your database. For each section, it returns the values of the fields you defined for the section’s document class. Key Information Extraction explains how it works.

Drivers of extraction accuracy

Section titled “Drivers of extraction accuracy”

Two things matter more than any setting.

Your definitions. The model reads each field’s description as its instruction. Ask for the values your database needs, keep values that belong together (a line item’s date, rate and description) in one list, say what each field is and isn’t (“the issue date, not the due date”), and give the format you want. See Improving accuracy.

Your annotations. A wrong annotation counts as an extraction error, and in our experience annotation errors are more than half of what Test Studio reports. Check each reported error and fix the annotation when the extraction was right. See Measure with ground truth.

The dataset behind most of these results. Most extraction results here come from RealKIE-FCC-Verified: real invoices with ground truth that people checked for every header field and every line item. We lean on it because its ground truth is accurate and its documents are real, varied layouts, not synthetic ones. It is one kind of document, broadcast advertising invoices, many of them with long lists of line items. If your documents have few or no line items, weigh the line-item results less and confirm on your own documents.

Results

Section titled “Results”
What the metrics mean
  • List recall: the share of ground-truth list cells extracted correctly; a lost row lowers it.
  • List precision: the share of extracted list cells that are correct; an invented or misaligned row lowers it.
  • Weighted accuracy: Stickler’s field-weighted score against ground truth.
  • Documents with every field right: the share of documents with no wrong field.
  • Completeness: the share of a list’s true rows that came back.
  • Accuracy over rows returned: the share of the values right in the rows that came back.

Weighted accuracy counts a whole list as one field, so a lost row barely moves it. Read list recall for lists.

Full definitions: Stickler’s Thresholds and Metrics and Aggregate Metrics, and Field Weighting in the evaluation guide.

Benchmark Datasets describes each dataset.

Agentic or single-pass extraction

Section titled “Agentic or single-pass extraction”

One point per invoice per extraction mode and model; each line is its running median.

Finding: Single-pass is a good place to start for most documents. Agentic extraction may do better on exceptionally long or complex extractions, such as invoices or receipts with many line items. On RealKIE invoices with more than about 100 line items, single-pass got almost no line items right, and agentic with Claude Haiku 5.5 got 44% to 79%. Below that, single-pass scored as well or better, for about the same cost or less. Agentic costs here are a floor: we can’t price its calls over 100,000 tokens, which Claude Haiku 5.5 charges more for. Test it on your own documents.

Details

Measured on each dataset’s preset, not the baseline setup.

Measured on release 0.6.15.dev1, November 2026, us-east-1. Source: 62 Test Studio runs (12 failed documents left out of the scores), listed in benchmarks/results/guidance/EX-01.json.

Single-pass scored as well as agentic or better up to 100 line items, and cost about the same there with the 5.5 models. Above 100 line items, agentic with Claude Haiku 5.5 scored as well as with Claude Sonnet 5.5 for at least $316 against $1,073 per 1,000 invoices; Claude Haiku 5.5’s agentic calls over 100,000 tokens bill at its long-context rate and can’t be priced from our metering, so its cost could be up to about two thirds of Claude Sonnet 5.5’s. Only 5 RealKIE invoices have more than 100 line items, and Test Studio could not score the longest (1,490 items) because its evaluation step timed out.

Extraction model

Section titled “Extraction model”

Bands show ±2 standard errors. Cost axes differ by dataset.

Finding: Claude Haiku 5.5 is a good place to start. On RealKIE invoices no model got a measurably higher weighted accuracy, and Claude Haiku 5.5 cost the least: $23 per 1,000 invoices, against $59 to $601 for the other Claude, OpenAI and xAI models. OpenAI GPT-6 Astra got every field right on the most invoices, 13% against none for Claude Haiku 5.5, for $601. Test it on your own documents.

The economics: On RealKIE invoices, OpenAI GPT-6 Astra scored no measurably better than Claude Haiku 5.5, so the extra $578 per 1,000 documents buys nothing measurable.

Document modality

Section titled “Document modality”

Bands show ±2 standard errors. Cost axes differ by dataset.

Finding: Page image plus OCR text is a good place to start. On W-2 forms and bank checks, OCR text alone scored lowest (W-2s 0.91 against 0.997; checks 0.89 against 0.994), and the page image alone got every field right on fewer forms (50% of W-2s against 82%). On RealKIE invoices all three scored about the same (0.77 to 0.79). Our image-only runs still paid for OCR; skipping it would save about $10 per 1,000 pages. Test it on your own documents.

Deterministic table parsing

Section titled “Deterministic table parsing”

One point per document per table parsing, a tool that turns Textract’s tables into rows by code instead of the model retyping them; each line is its running median.

Finding: Off is a good place to start. On 18 long RealKIE invoices (8 to 33 pages), agentic extraction with table parsing on scored no better than with it off (line-item score 52% against 56%), cost more (at least $257 against $210 per 1,000 invoices) and took longer. Test it on your own long tables.

Long lists

Section titled “Long lists”

One small point per document per run, for each extraction mode and table parsing; the large mark is the mean of those runs at each size, matching the table, and each line joins the means.

Finding: Agentic extraction is a good place to start for long lists. On synthetic statements with Claude Haiku 5.5, single-pass returned every row at 100 rows, but from 400 rows some statements came back with only the first page’s rows (64% of rows at 400 rows, 2% at 1,600), and Amazon Bedrock rejected the 3,200-row requests as too long. Agentic extraction returned every row at every size with table parsing on, and 98% to 100% with it off. On these clean tables, table parsing also made agentic extraction cheaper: from 800 rows it cost less than single-pass even if every call billed at the long-context rate. Real invoices agree on agentic extraction, which found 67% of the line items against 17% for single-pass (Agentic or single-pass extraction), but not on table parsing, which didn’t help on long real invoices (Deterministic table parsing). Test it on your own documents.

The economics: On 800-row statements, agentic extraction with table parsing instead of single-pass saves $128 of extraction per 1,000 documents and gets 302,800 more rows right.

Schema validation and value coercion

Section titled “Schema validation and value coercion”

Bands show ±2 standard errors.

Finding: Keep both on; they’re a cheap safety net. Validation checks each value against the schema and records what fails. Coercion converts values to the schema’s types, for example “$6.00” to 6.0. On RealKIE invoices, accuracy was the same with them on or off for every model, and they added up to 8% to the cost. Coercion changed no value, and validation flagged only Amazon Nova 2 Lite: 5 of 60 sections were missing a required field, which it records but doesn’t fix.
